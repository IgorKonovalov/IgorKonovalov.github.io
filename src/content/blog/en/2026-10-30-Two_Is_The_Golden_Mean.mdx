Not long ago I met a friend, a former colleague from my dream team, from the time of the WYSIWYG editor. She works on another project now, also with agents; they don't write code by hand either. And she told me a story. There were four of them, and then management decided to hire three more developers, so there were seven. And after those three were hired, she said, their work became much slower than when they were four.

I asked what that looked like in practice. More meetings, and longer ones. Too much communication. Approvals. Every pull request waits for one person to approve it. People stepping on each other's toes. And a lot of planning — a lot. They don't really write that much code, she said. Mostly they sit in meetings and wait for each other. As far as I know, the team's delivery slowed down.

I see the same: when there are more people, the overhead on communication eats the velocity that could be there. In the [previous post](/personal/2026/10/29/half_a_year_of_work_in_a_week_and_a_half/) two developers and an analyst did about half a year of work in a week and a half. This one is about why an ordinary scrum team won't get this speed even with the same agents. It is the most opinionated post of the series: there is a forecast here, and a forecast can be wrong. But it is what I think.

## Where the time went

Why do I think classic scrum no longer fits this kind of work? Because in reality we spent no more than 30–40% of our time writing code, and leads and seniors even less. A huge amount of time went into passing information between the members of the team.

My day as a lead from the [first post](/personal/2026/10/28/ten_years_of_writing_code_by_hand/): four or five hours of meetings back to back, up to 70% of my time on calls and approvals. I didn't hate it — I liked talking to people. But the code was the smaller part of the job, and that was true for most of the people around me.

This is not only my experience. In [Atlassian's 2025 survey](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/developer/developer-experience-report-2025) of 3,500 developers, half said they lose ten or more hours a week to friction that has nothing to do with writing code, and 63% said their leaders don't understand their pain points.

Agents make almost free exactly the smaller part — the code. The meetings, the approvals, the handovers and the waiting stay. If code was a third of your time, even infinitely fast code saves only that third; the big studies, which I will come back to, find twenty or thirty percent.

I don't see how meetings and ceremonies can be made fast enough to keep pace with the agents — especially if we also invite the agents to them.

## The arithmetic of people

My friend's story has simple arithmetic behind it, and it is not new. Every pair of people in a team is a channel through which information has to pass: something to agree, to explain, to wait for. With _n_ people there are _n_(_n_ − 1) / 2 such pairs. Fred Brooks wrote about this in 1975, in _The Mythical Man-Month_: adding people to a late project makes it later.

From four to seven people is less than twice the people and more than three times the lines. The agents only make this arithmetic worse: each of the seven produces more changes that the other six have to know about, review, merge with their own, and talk about in a meeting. The faster the code is written, the more the communication costs relative to it.

When I look at our team, it is the opposite. Two people, one line. Everything I need to agree, I agree with one person. Everything he needs to know about my work, he can read in the summary that the agent wrote for the pull request.

## Decisions that wait

More than meetings, what slows a big team down is decisions. There were many cases when we waited to show a UX design demo to the customer, the design wasn't approved, and we had to redo it, and then get it approved again. Once I worked on a project where every change in the code had to go through the customer's architect. At one point that even meant screenshots by email. That project showed me what a slow approval loop costs, and I moved on soon after.

In big bureaucratic machines a decision can stay for many months. I have seen such projects. And I think what gets lost there is one simple thing: it is easier to implement a product and implement it again later, if something goes wrong, than to approve it for months.

With us, decisions look very different. The decision to move the backend from Java to TypeScript was one hour of talk. The decision to upgrade all the outdated packages and move from npm to pnpm as the package manager, I made myself, and only approved it through my colleague. The change was implemented within a couple of hours, tested with e2e tests, and would have been just as easy to roll back. When a rollback is cheap, planning far ahead becomes unnecessary.

Another example. The other developer made a dashboard with search facets. The customer didn't like it. I redid it from the feedback within an hour, end to end, and it was already on the dev stand for the customer and everyone else to see. In the past such work would take at least two weeks; now it was an hour, with no friction at all.

When a change costs an hour, you don't need to approve it before you make it. You make it, show it, and if it is wrong you make it again. Approval was a way to protect expensive work from being wasted. The work is not expensive anymore, but the approval is still there, and now it is the most expensive part.

So far this is my experience and one story from someone else. The measurements in big companies say the same thing.

- [Google, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944): a randomised experiment with 96 engineers; with AI they finished a task about 21% faster.
- [Cui and colleagues, 2025](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4945566): the experiments with almost five thousand developers from the previous post, 26% more completed tasks. This was code completion, the tools of the previous generation, not agents.
- [METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/): sixteen experienced open-source developers were 19% _slower_ with AI — and believed afterwards that they had been 20% faster. In a [rerun at the end of 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) the picture changed toward a speed-up, but METR itself says the data is unreliable and the speed-up is likely underestimated: developers refused to work without AI, and the parallel agents broke the time logging.
- [Bain, 2025](https://www.bain.com/insights/from-pilots-to-payoff-generative-ai-in-software-development-technology-report-2025/): typical companies get 10–15%. Writing code and testing are only 25–35% of the way from an idea to a launch. Companies that redesign the whole process get 25–30%.

Bain's 25–35% is almost my 30–40%, only counted from the outside: that third is the only part you can speed up, and everything else is the process.

And then there is what happens when you speed up only the code. [Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering) looked at more than ten thousand developers: with AI, 98% more pull requests were merged — and review time grew by 91%, pull requests became 154% bigger, and at the level of the company there was no significant effect on throughput or quality. That is the single approver from my friend's story, measured on ten thousand people.

The sentence I like most is from the [DORA 2025 report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report): _"AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there."_ A team that spends two thirds of its time in meetings gets agents and spends two thirds of its time in meetings. A team that can just take and do things gets agents and does them ten times faster.

## My own number

Nobody has measured my own number: it is my estimate of what an old team would need for the same work, divided by a week and a half. There is no controlled study of small teams with agents — only claims from investors: Y Combinator said in March 2025 that for a quarter of its winter batch, 95% of the code was written by AI. So the last bar on the chart is not science. It is what I see with my own eyes.

And there are reasons not to trust it. The METR study showed that developers overestimate their own speed-up, and I am a developer too. None of this code is in production yet; we are waiting for access. It is one week and a half on one project, and it is a legacy rewrite: the old application already tells us the right answer, and we compare the result byte for byte. New features with unclear requirements will not go this fast, so read seventeen as what a rewrite gives, not as a promise for every project. But even if I am wrong several times over, it is far more than thirty percent.

To check myself, I took two pet projects, where git keeps the real days, and estimated how long a senior would need to build them by hand.

The first is a Telegram bot for personal expenses, the one we use at home every day. You type a short line, an amount and a word, and it records the expense; it reads Serbian and Montenegrin fiscal receipts from a QR code, bank SMS and PDF statements, converts currencies at the central bank rate of the day, keeps budgets, recurring expenses, debts and shared ledgers for group chats, and draws charts in a Telegram Mini App. With agents it took 13 days of work in about two weeks, in three bursts, from the end of September to the middle of October. By hand, broken down feature by feature, with the tests and the decision records it has, I estimate it at 80–90 working days, about four months. That is about six to seven times.

The second is the voxel engine, only the engine. With agents it took nine days, from the 16th to the 28th of October. By hand, more than a year — and that is for someone who knows Rust well; for me it would be longer. That is twenty-five times or more.

It looks strange: the bot is much simpler, and it took more days. But the days are not the same: on the bot I spent much less than a full day each time, and the engine already ran with full orchestration, so a day of work on it was a different kind of day.

An ordinary product comes out six or seven times faster, a hard one twenty-five, and the rewrite at work, at seventeen, sits between them. All of it with a team of one or two people.

## No silver bullet

Why do the studies find thirty percent, and we see an order of magnitude? The answer is Brooks again. In 1986 he wrote [_No Silver Bullet_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Silver_Bullet): _"There is no single development, in either technology or management technique, which by itself promises even one order-of-magnitude improvement within a decade in productivity, in reliability, in simplicity."_ He split the difficulty of software into two parts. The accidental part comes from our tools: typing the code, the languages, the machines. The essential part is in the problem itself: understanding what to build, the specification, the design, agreeing about it.

Agents are not the silver bullet either. They removed a big piece of the accidental part — writing code by hand. The essential part stayed, and in a big team a large share of it is people agreeing with each other. That is why a scrum team with agents gets its thirty percent. The order of magnitude I see came from two developments at once — in Brooks's terms, a technology and a management technique: the agents and a team of two with complete freedom. Neither of them alone would do it.

## Why our team is the exception

Any team can be given agents; the second development is harder. We are two developers, both full-stack, and both understand what they are doing. We don't step on each other's toes, we want to work fast, and we decide ourselves how to do the work. If any of these things were missing, it wouldn't work. If we had eight people now and scrum, we couldn't do anything even close to what we do.

Where does the freedom come from? The client judges us by a working product, not by how we organise ourselves, and EPAM lets us choose how we work. I would say this is a rare case.

That is when a small team of developers who know what they are doing and want to do it well can move not 20–30% faster, but ten times, a hundred times faster than without agents. But it needs a different approach to management and a very high level of trust in the team.

A harder question: would my dream team, the people I loved working with, get there with today's agents and our old scrum? I don't think so. Only if it were divided. We were too big, with too much communication overhead.

## Two is the golden mean

Why two and not one? Two are needed to check each other: the other developer has a different context, and several times, from the summary or the agent's review, he saw that something was wrong. Just today he caught that, under some circumstances, our login page was inaccessible for users who were not logged in — something I was not aware of. One developer with agents has no one to do that.

Three is already a lot, and more than three, for this kind of work, I don't see a need for. Two is the golden mean, a magic number.

## What is left of the lead

I was a lead for five years and I loved it. One function of it is left — answering for decisions: when a decision is spread over several people, no one is responsible. The lead is the one who is asked. All the other functions of a lead — coordination and the rest — move into the background: there is no one to manage, the people are two, and the rest are agents.

We have no formal lead, but the decisions are split. The other developer owns what we build for the customer and how it looks; I own the technical side: the harness, the quality, the tests. For each kind of decision there is one person.

## What is left of scrum

What is left of the ceremonies is high-level planning: what layer of work is ahead — rewriting the legacy on a new stack, say. We estimate only very roughly, if at all: almost anything can be done. The work goes faster than we imagine, and even we don't really understand how fast we are moving. We are still adjusting: in the past we underestimated our work, now we overestimate it.

And we grow the bonsai from the previous post: the next step becomes visible only after the previous one is done, so a detailed plan is impossible.

Sprints are not needed either: we do the work until it is ready, show a demo to the customer, then hold a retro. Our retro is more a little party where we talk about everything than a formal meeting, but I like retros and think they are needed: problems have to be said out loud so they can be solved.

Dailies are probably not needed: ad hoc messages and the Trello board replace the dailies quite well. Sometimes we had dailies and sometimes we didn't. Sometimes it was just a virtual office, and that suits me fine.

## The bottleneck that is left

What bottlenecks are left? Integration. We are waiting for passwords and access: we have the code, but we can't deploy it.

How does it feel? Calm. As long as there is work I can do, I do it. Access is outside my control, and I feel no anxiety about it. If they give it in a month, then it is in a month.

I think this is worth saying, because it is the same decisions that wait, only outside. Inside our team the bottlenecks are removed; outside it, the world works at the old speed. Our two-person team plugs into a big machine — access, tickets, other teams — and at the joint the machine's speed wins. Even inside the project the two worlds sit side by side: the client has Jira with formal stories, we have our Trello board.

## Renovation crews

How could teams like ours look in the future? I think just like ours: two developers and one person who removes obstacles, talks to the business and answers for the business decisions. This person can help a lot to speed things up. He doesn't write code, but with him the developers don't have to look back and ask how to do this or that. You just take it and do it.

I have a picture in my head for this, and it comes from my life before IT. In the first post I wrote about the gypsum stucco, about how our crew often came after other craftsmen and redid it all. We were a crew of three. We were seen and known as high-quality professionals, and we always had work to do. All of it came by word of mouth.

Programmers, I think, will go the same way. Not a nameless contractor that you give a task to and forget, but a team with faces that advertises itself through social media: here we are, we make this kind of thing. Superstars with medals around their necks who come and do it, like a SWAT team.

I have seen this with renovation crews, never with programmers: there are star developers on Twitter, but not crews. Only in the right hands does an LLM speed up development enormously — so those hands should be seen.

And such a crew doesn't stay forever: it comes in, builds, and leaves for another project. Teams that work together for years, I think, will be gone, and big teams too.

## Who will ride the wave

That is the answer to the question I started with. My friend's team slowed down even though it has agents: the agents sped up only the code, and the seven people, the twenty-one lines between them and the single approver are all still there. The future, I think, belongs to small teams, and the companies that manage to give them autonomy, a minimum of constraints and high trust will be on the crest of the wave. For a big organisation this is the hardest change: its processes were built for a time when code was expensive. Companies that keep big teams exactly as they are will struggle — the bureaucratic pyramid will most likely have to be broken up.

What we have can be repeated. It is easiest in a small team or a startup, but possible in a big company too, if someone deliberately gives a team this freedom: that is how it happened with us. And such teams will move at such a speed that everyone else's ears will pop and their eyes will flicker, because they will deliver something incredible.

And, like every time I came into this profession, I got lucky again: with what is happening around me now, and that I can work at a velocity like this. But this speed has a price — the last post is about it.
