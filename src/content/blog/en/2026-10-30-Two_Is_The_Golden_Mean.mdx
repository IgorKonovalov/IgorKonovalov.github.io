Not long ago I met a friend, a former colleague from my dream team, from the time of the WYSIWYG editor. She works on another project now, also with agents; they don't write code by hand either. And she told me a story. There were four of them, and then management decided to hire three more developers, so there were seven. And after those three were hired, she said, their work became much slower than when they were four.

I asked what that looked like in practice. More meetings, and longer ones. Too much communication. Approvals. Every pull request waits for one person to approve it. People stepping on each other's toes. And a lot of planning — a lot. They don't really write that much code, she said. Mostly they sit in meetings and wait for each other. As far as I know, the team's delivery slowed down.

This matches exactly what I see myself: when there are more people, the overhead on communication, on meetings, on ceremonies goes past what is acceptable, and the velocity that could be there just doesn't happen. In the [previous post](/personal/2026/10/29/half_a_year_of_work_in_a_week_and_a_half/) I wrote that two developers and an analyst did about half a year of work in a week and a half, and I promised to explain why an ordinary scrum team won't get this speed even with the same agents. This is that post. It is the most opinionated of the four: there is a forecast here, and a forecast can be wrong. But it is what I think.

## Where the time went

Why do I think classic scrum no longer fits this kind of work? Because in reality we spent no more than 30–40% of our time writing code. Especially in the lead position, in the senior position. A huge amount of time went into passing information between the members of the team.

I described my day as a lead in the [first post](/personal/2026/10/28/ten_years_of_writing_code_by_hand/): four or five hours of meetings back to back, code review and messages in between. Calls and approvals took up to 70% of my time. I didn't hate it — I liked talking to people. But the code was the smaller part of the job, and that was true for most of the people around me.

This is not only my experience. In [Atlassian's 2025 survey](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/developer/developer-experience-report-2025) of 3,500 developers, half said they lose ten or more hours a week to friction that has nothing to do with writing code, and 63% said their leaders don't understand their pain points.

Now look at what agents do. They take the part that was already the smaller one, writing code, and make it almost free. Everything else stays: the meetings, the approvals, the handovers, the waiting. If code was a third of your time, then even if it became infinitely fast, you could save only that third, never more. The other two thirds stay. The work cannot become ten times faster this way; the big studies, which I will come back to, find twenty or thirty percent.

I don't see how meetings and ceremonies can be made fast enough to keep pace with the agents — especially if we also invite the agents to them.

## The arithmetic of people

My friend's story has simple arithmetic behind it, and it is not new. Every pair of people in a team is a channel through which information has to pass: something to agree, to explain, to wait for. With _n_ people there are _n_(_n_ − 1) / 2 such pairs. Fred Brooks wrote about this in 1975, in _The Mythical Man-Month_: adding people to a late project makes it later.

From four to seven people is less than twice the people and more than three times the lines between them. The agents don't change this number at all. Each of the seven developers now writes code faster, and so each of them produces more changes that the other six have to know about, review, merge with their own, and talk about in a meeting. The faster the code is written, the more the communication costs relative to it.

When I look at our team, it is the opposite. Two people, one line. Everything I need to agree, I agree with one person. Everything he needs to know about my work, he can read in the summary that the agent wrote for my pull request.

## Decisions that wait

But the biggest thing that slows a big team down is not code and not even meetings. It is decisions.

There were many cases when we waited to show a UX design demo to the customer, the design wasn't approved, and we had to redo it, and then get it approved again. Once I worked on a project where every change in the code had to go through the customer's architect. At one point that even meant screenshots by email. That project showed me what a slow approval loop costs, and I moved on soon after.

In big bureaucratic machines a decision can stay for many months. I have seen such projects. And I think what gets lost there is one simple thing: it is easier to implement a product and implement it again later, if something goes wrong, than to approve it for months.

With us, decisions look very different.

The decision to move the backend from Java to TypeScript was one hour of talk. The decision to upgrade all the outdated packages and move from npm to pnpm as the package manager, I made myself, and only approved it through my colleague. The change was implemented within a couple of hours and tested with e2e tests and everything else, so it was easy to do and easy to roll back in case something failed. This is the important part: the ease of implementation gives you the freedom to roll back if needed. And then planning far ahead becomes unnecessary.

Another example. The other developer made a dashboard with search facets. The customer didn't like it. I redid it from the feedback within an hour, end to end, and it was already on the dev stand for the customer and everyone else to see. In the past such work would take at least two weeks: a designer designs it, the design goes for approval, the work is planned, split into subtasks, delegated, implemented, tested, and eventually shipped. Now it was an hour, with no friction at all.

When a change costs an hour, you don't need to approve it before you make it. You make it, show it, and if it is wrong you make it again. Approval was a way to protect expensive work from being wasted. The work is not expensive anymore, but the approval is still there, and now it is the most expensive part.

## What the research measures

So far this is my experience and one story from someone else, but there are measurements too. When I wrote in the previous post that we see a thousand percent and more, I put next to it the numbers from enterprise research: twenty or thirty percent. Here they are in more detail, because I think they say the same thing as my friend's story.

- [Google, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944): a randomised experiment with 96 engineers; with AI they finished a task about 21% faster.
- [Cui and colleagues, 2025](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4945566): the experiments with almost five thousand developers from the previous post, 26% more completed tasks. This was code completion, the tools of the previous generation, not agents.
- [METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/): sixteen experienced open-source developers were 19% _slower_ with AI — and believed afterwards that they had been 20% faster. In a [rerun at the end of 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) the picture changed toward a speed-up, but METR itself says the data is unreliable and the speed-up is likely underestimated: developers refused to work without AI, and the parallel agents broke the time logging.
- [Bain, 2025](https://www.bain.com/insights/from-pilots-to-payoff-generative-ai-in-software-development-technology-report-2025/): typical companies get 10–15%. Writing code and testing are only 25–35% of the way from an idea to a launch. Companies that redesign the whole process get 25–30%.

Bain's 25–35% is almost my 30–40%, only counted from the outside. It is the same third I wrote about above: that is the only part you can speed up, and everything else is the process.

And then there is what happens when you speed up only the code. [Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering) looked at more than ten thousand developers: with AI, 98% more pull requests were merged — and review time grew by 91%, pull requests became 154% bigger, and at the level of the company there was no significant effect on throughput or quality. More code went into the same pipe, and the pipe didn't get wider. That is the single approver from my friend's story, only measured on ten thousand people.

The sentence I like most is from the [DORA 2025 report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report): _"AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there."_ A team that spends two thirds of its time in meetings gets agents and spends two thirds of its time in meetings. A team that can just take and do things gets agents and does them ten times faster.

## My own number

I want to be honest about my own number, too. Nobody has measured it. It is my estimate of what an old team would need for the same work, divided by a week and a half. And there is no controlled study of small teams that work with agents from the start; all I found about them are claims from investors — for example, Y Combinator said in March 2025 that for a quarter of its winter batch, 95% of the code was written by AI. So the last bar on the chart is not science. It is what I see with my own eyes, and there are good reasons not to trust it too much.

The METR study showed that developers overestimate their own speed-up: they were slower and believed they were faster. I am a developer too. None of this code is in production yet; we are still waiting for access. It is one week and a half on one project. And it is a rewrite, from legacy to a modern stack, and with agents that is much easier: the old application already tells us what the right answer is, and we compare the result byte for byte. New features with unclear requirements will not go this fast. So read seventeen as what a rewrite gives, not as a promise for every project. But even if I am wrong several times over, it is still far more than thirty percent, and it does not come from the agents alone.

I wanted at least one more check, so I took two of my pet projects, where git keeps the real days, and estimated how long one senior developer would need to build them by hand.

The first is a Telegram bot for personal expenses, the one we use at home every day. You type a short line, an amount and a word, and it records the expense; it reads Serbian and Montenegrin fiscal receipts from a QR code, bank SMS and PDF statements, converts currencies at the central bank rate of the day, keeps budgets, recurring expenses, debts and shared ledgers for group chats, and draws charts in a Telegram Mini App. With agents it took 13 days of work in about two weeks, in three bursts, from the end of September to the middle of October. By hand, broken down feature by feature, with the tests and the decision records it has, I estimate it at 80–90 working days, about four months. That is about six to seven times.

The second is the voxel engine, only the engine. With agents it took nine days, from the 16th to the 28th of October. By hand I think it is more than a year — and only if I knew Rust very well. That is twenty-five times or more, and that is an estimate for a specialist; for me as I am, it would be longer.

It looks strange: the bot is much simpler, and it took more days than the engine. But the days are not the same. On the bot I spent much less than a full day each time. The engine already ran with full orchestration, so a day of work on it was a different kind of day.

So my own numbers are not one number. An ordinary product comes out around six or seven times, a hard and specialised one around twenty-five, and the rewrite at work, at seventeen, sits between them. All of it with a team of one or two people.

## No silver bullet

Why do the studies find thirty percent, and we see an order of magnitude? Fred Brooks has a second text that explains it, and it fits here even better than _The Mythical Man-Month_. In 1986 he wrote [_No Silver Bullet_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Silver_Bullet): _"There is no single development, in either technology or management technique, which by itself promises even one order-of-magnitude improvement within a decade in productivity, in reliability, in simplicity."_ He split the difficulty of software into two parts. The accidental part comes from our tools: typing the code, the languages, the machines. The essential part is in the problem itself: understanding what to build, the specification, the design, agreeing about it.

Agents are not the silver bullet either. They removed a big piece of the accidental part — writing code by hand. The essential part stayed exactly where it was, and in a big team a large share of it is people agreeing with each other. That is why a scrum team with agents gets its thirty percent. Where I see an order of magnitude, it did not come from one development. It came from two at once, in his own words: a technology, the agents, and a management technique, a team of two with complete freedom. Neither of them alone would do it.

## Why our team is the exception

Any team can be given agents; the second development is harder. Why does it work for us? Again, because the team is very small — only two developers. Both understand what they are doing. Both are full-stack and understand the backend and the frontend well. We don't step on each other's toes: we work together, we want to work, and we want to work fast. And we have complete freedom: we decide ourselves how to do the work, and everyone understands what to do. If any of these things were missing, we couldn't work like this. If we had eight people now and scrum, we couldn't do anything even close to what we do.

Where does the freedom come from? The client judges us by a working product, not by how we organise ourselves, and EPAM lets us choose how we work. I would say this is a rare case.

So what really works is a small, concrete team of developers who know what they are doing and want to do it well. Then we really can move ten times faster — not 20–30%, but ten times, a hundred times faster than without agents. But it needs a completely different approach to management and a very high level of trust in the team. Only like that.

There is a harder question, too. My dream team, the people I loved working with: if we got today's agents but kept our old scrum, would we get there? I don't think so. Only if such a team were divided. We were too big, with too much communication overhead.

## Two is the golden mean

Why two and not one? Because there must be two, so that they can check each other. Several times the other developer saw something that was wrong. He has a different context. He reads the summary or the agent's review and understands that something is not right. Just today he caught that, under some circumstances, our login page was inaccessible for users who were not logged in — something I was not aware of. One developer with agents has no one to do that.

Three is already a lot. Two is the golden mean between one and three. More than three, for this kind of work, I don't see a need for. So two is a magic number for me.

## What is left of the lead

I was a lead for five years and I loved it. But I think the person who makes decisions and is responsible for them must be one concrete person. When a decision is spread over several people, no one is responsible. So some lead should exist, at least formally — exactly for these decisions and for the responsibility. I think that is now the main function of a lead: this person answers for what we do. They are the one who is asked.

All the other functions of a lead — coordination and the rest — move into the background, because they are not really needed. There is no one to manage: the people are two, and the rest are agents.

We have no formal lead. But in practice the decisions are split. The other developer owns the decisions for the customer: what we build and how it looks to them. I own everything technical: the harness, the quality, the tests. So in our team the responsibility is not spread either: for each kind of decision there is one concrete person, and each of the two of us owns his side.

## What is left of scrum

What is left of the ceremonies? Very high-level planning. We roughly understand what layer of work we need to do: we will rewrite the legacy on new technologies, we will do this and that. And we don't really estimate anything anymore, only very roughly. Almost anything can be done. We are still adjusting, because the work goes faster than we imagine. Even we don't really understand how fast we are moving — much faster than before. If in the past the problem was that we underestimated our work, now we overestimate it.

And because we grow this bonsai — I wrote about it in the previous post — the real work becomes visible only when we start it. So the planning cannot be detailed anyway: the next step appears after the previous one is done.

So I think sprints are not really needed in this context. We just do the work up to the moment when it is ready, show a demo to the customer, and then hold a retro. Our retro is more a little party where we just talk about everything, not a formal retro. But a retro is good, feedback is good. I like retros. If we had some problems, we need to say them out loud to each other, so that they can be solved. So I think a retro is needed.

Dailies — probably not. Ad hoc messages and the Trello board replace the dailies quite well. Sometimes we had dailies and sometimes we didn't. Sometimes it was just a virtual office, and that suits me fine.

## The bottleneck that is left

What bottlenecks are left? Integration. Right now we are waiting for passwords, waiting for access. We have the code, but we can't deploy it.

How does it feel? Calm. As long as there is work I can do, I do it. Access is outside my control, and I have no reason to hurry or to hurry anyone — in the sense that I feel no anxiety about it. If they give it in a month, then it is in a month.

I think this is worth saying, because it is the same decisions that wait, only outside. Inside our team every bottleneck is removed. Outside it, the world still works at the old speed: access, tickets, other teams. Our two-person team plugs into a big machine, and at the joint the machine's speed wins. Even inside the project the two worlds sit side by side: the client has Jira with formal stories, and we have our Trello board, which the client doesn't see.

## Renovation crews

How could teams like ours look in the future? Well, just like our team: two developers. Plus one person who removes obstacles, talks to the business, takes on the responsibility — maybe for exactly the business decisions. This person can help a lot to speed things up. He will not write code, but he will help the developers work so that they don't have to look back all the time and ask someone how to do this or that. You just take it and do it.

I have a picture in my head for this, and it comes from my life before IT. In the first post I wrote about the gypsum stucco, about how our crew often came after other craftsmen who had done it badly, and redid it. We were a crew of three. We were seen and known as high-quality professionals, and we always had work to do. All of it came by word of mouth.

I think programmers will go somewhere there. It will not be a nameless contractor that you give a task to and forget about. It will be a team with faces. A team with faces that advertises itself through social media, probably like this: here we are, such great guys, we make this kind of thing. Superstars with medals around their necks who come and do it, like a SWAT team.

I have seen this with renovation crews, but never with programmers, so it is only my prediction. There are some star developers on Twitter, but not crews. But here is the thing: in the right hands an LLM can speed up development enormously — only in the right hands. So those hands should be seen.

And such a crew doesn't stay forever. It comes in, sets something up or builds it, and leaves for another project — like a crew on a renovation or construction job. I think teams that work together for years will be gone, and big teams too.

## Who will ride the wave

That is the answer to the question I started with. My friend's team slowed down even though it has agents: the agents sped up only the code, and the seven people, the twenty-one lines between them and the single approver of every pull request are all still there. So I think the future belongs to small, concrete teams, and the companies that manage to ride this future will be on the crest of the wave. For a big organisation this is the hardest change: its processes were built for a time when code was expensive, and its bureaucratic structure is often so rigid that it doesn't allow people that level of freedom.

What will happen to companies that keep big teams exactly as they are? I think they will struggle. Something will have to be done with the bureaucratic pyramid — most likely, breaking it up into small teams.

Without serious changes in the approach to work — autonomous teams, a minimum of management, a minimum of constraints, high trust — such work is impossible. The question is how to make developers as free as possible, so that they can do creative work and, roughly speaking, sell their creative work, with nothing unnecessary getting in their way.

Can what we have be repeated somewhere else? I think yes. It is easiest to start in a small team or a startup, but it is possible in a big company too, if someone deliberately gives a team this freedom — that is how it happened with us. And such teams will move at such a speed that everyone else's ears will pop and their eyes will flicker, because they will deliver something incredible.

And again, it seems to me that I am lucky — like the previous times, when I was getting into this path, into this profession. I got lucky again with what is happening around me now, and I can work at a real velocity like this.

But luck is only one side of it. Everything I wrote here about small teams, freedom and fast work has a price, and not only for companies. The next post, the last one, is about that: what it costs me, and what it may cost the people who are only starting.
